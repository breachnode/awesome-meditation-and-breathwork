# Awesome Meditation & Breathwork

**A quiet corner of the internet. Simple ways to slow down, breathe easier, and feel a little more like yourself — all of them free.**

No experience needed. No gear to buy. Nothing to believe. Just a few quiet minutes and your own breath.

This guide gathers the simplest, best-loved practices from teachers and researchers around the world: ways to sit, ways to breathe, and small habits of gratitude. Every technique here is free, and the free tools come before the paid ones. Take what helps. Leave the rest. Come back whenever you need a slower minute.

**Breachnode Field Guide № 5.** Kept by [Breachnode](https://github.com/breachnode) — knowledge shared in the open, on his own time.

> 🧘 **Three small skills — that's all this is.**
> **Meditation** is resting your attention on one thing — your breath, your body, a kind word — and gently bringing it back when it wanders. That bringing-back is the whole exercise. **Breathwork** is using your breath on purpose: breathe slowly, and your body gets the message that it's safe to relax. **Gratitude** is noticing what's good, on purpose, for a minute or two. None of it takes talent. A few minutes a day is a real dose.

## 🚀 Start Here

- 📋 **Your first two minutes.** Sit somewhere comfortable and set a timer for two minutes. Breathe normally, and rest your attention on the feeling of the breath — cool air coming in, warm air going out. When you notice you're thinking about something else (you will, within seconds), don't scold yourself. That's not failure; that's the rep. Gently come back to the breath. Repeat until the timer ends. That is meditation. Everything else in this guide is a variation on it.
- 📋 **"Meditation doesn't work for me."** If you've tried and quit, it probably went like this: you sat down, tried to force your mind blank, and thirty seconds later you were grading yourself — and failing a test nobody was running. Hear this clearly: there is nothing to force. You can't force yourself to fall asleep either. You lie down, you get comfortable, and you let it happen. Same here. There's no score, no streak to protect, no finish line. When your mind wanders — it will — noticing that *is* the practice. The wandering mind isn't the problem. It's the workout.
- 📋 **You don't have to clear your mind.** Let that go early. A session where you notice your mind wandered twenty times is twenty gentle reps. Blank silence isn't the goal, and mostly it isn't on offer.
- 📋 **A little, often.** Five quiet minutes most days will do more than one long session on a Sunday. Small and steady is how every skill grows — this one included.
- **[Relaxation Techniques for Health — NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/relaxation-techniques-for-health)** — A friendly page from the National Institutes of Health on the "relaxation response" — your body's built-in way of standing down: slower breathing, lower heart rate, loosened muscles. It covers deep breathing, guided imagery, and more, in plain language. A good read when you want the basics from a source that isn't selling anything. `Free`

## 🧘 Ways to Meditate

Many doors, same room. Try a few, and keep the ones that fit your life.

- 📋 **Focused attention — the basic.** Pick one anchor: the breath at your nose, a count, a candle flame. Rest your attention on it. Your mind wanders. You notice. You return. That's all, and that's everything. Start with 5–10 minutes. When you catch a thought, label it silently — "thinking" — and go back to the anchor.
- **[Mindfulness / MBSR — Palouse Mindfulness (free online course)](https://palousemindfulness.com)** — The most-taught program in the world, free online. Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction is an 8-week course built at the University of Massachusetts Medical School, and Palouse offers the whole thing — videos, readings, guided audio — with no fees and no sign-up. The practice: follow the breath, notice thoughts and body feelings without arguing with them, return. A real curriculum, at the price this guide prefers. `Free`
- **[Body scan — UCLA Mindful Awareness Research Center (MARC)](https://www.uclahealth.org/marc/)** — Attention walked slowly through the body, part by part — feet, legs, belly, chest, hands, face — feeling what's actually there. UCLA's center posts free guided body scans in English and Spanish, and its UCLA Mindful app is free too. Lovely before sleep, and a kind first practice if sitting still feels hard. `Free`
- 📋 **Open monitoring.** Focused attention with the anchor taken away. Sit, and let whatever shows up — sounds, sensations, thoughts — arrive and leave without grabbing any of it. Start with two minutes of breath focus to settle, then open up. When you get hooked by a thought, that's the rep: unhook, and open up again. A good next step once the basic feels friendly.
- **[Loving-kindness (metta) — Galante et al. (2014)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24979314/)** — Deliberately wishing well. Silently repeat simple phrases — "may I be safe, may I be well" — first toward yourself, then a benefactor, a neutral person, and gradually everyone. It comes out of Buddhist practice; the lab version keeps the technique, not the doctrine. It can feel awkward at first, especially toward yourself. Awkward is a starting point, not a verdict. `Free`
- 📋 **Mantra.** Sit comfortably, eyes closed, and repeat one word or sound silently — any word you like. Let thoughts come and go around it. Fifteen to twenty minutes is a classic dose, but five is a fine start. Simple, portable, quiet.
- **[Transcendental Meditation — course fee (official)](https://usa.tm.org/course-fee)** — The trademarked version of mantra practice: taught one-on-one by certified teachers over four sessions, with a personal mantra assigned. The honest price, from TM's own fee page: about **$540–$980** depending on household income, $420 for full-time students. Some people genuinely do better with a teacher and a set system, and there's no shame in paying for one. Just know the basic technique — a quietly repeated word — is the free entry above. `Paid`
- **[Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) — Kuyken et al., JAMA Psychiatry (2016)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27119968/)** — Mindfulness training blended with cognitive-therapy tools, taught as an 8-week course. It's known for helping people who have had repeated depression stay well longer. This one is a program you take with a trained teacher, often alongside your own clinician — treat it as a course, not a solo practice, and it can be a real support. `Free`
- 📋 **Visualization / guided imagery.** Close your eyes and build a calm place in detail — somewhere you feel safe — and walk through it slowly, senses first: what you see, hear, smell, feel. Or picture yourself calmly handling the thing you're dreading. Good for visual thinkers, and before anything that makes you nervous.
- 📋 **Walking meditation.** Meditation with the legs — for the days when sitting still simply isn't happening. Walk slowly — ten paces, turn, ten paces — with all your attention on the mechanics: lifting, moving, placing, the shift of weight. Ten minutes is a full session. A real practice, not a lesser one.
- **[Yoga — NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/yoga-what-you-need-to-know)** — Movement, breathing, and meditation woven into one practice. It began in India as a spiritual practice; the version in most gyms keeps the postures, the breath, and the meditation. Styles run from gentle to athletic — start gentle. The calm doesn't require the hard poses, whatever the photos suggest. `Free`
- **[Tai chi & qigong — NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/tai-chi-in-depth)** — Slow, flowing movements with controlled breathing and a settled mind. Tai chi began in China as a martial art before it became a health practice. It's easy on the joints, kind to balance, and a favorite for older adults — and for anyone who meditates better while moving. Qigong is its simpler cousin: fewer, repeated movements, same principles. `Free`

## 🌬️ Breathing Exercises

Your breath is the one dial you can always reach. Slow it down, and your heart follows it down. Start with the gentle ones — they're the daily drivers.

- **[Belly breathing — Ma et al., Frontiers in Psychology (2017)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28626434/)** — Learn this one first; every other pattern stands on it. One hand on your chest, one on your belly. Breathe in through your nose so the *belly* hand rises and the chest hand barely moves. Breathe out slowly through your mouth. Five minutes, a few times a day, until belly-first breathing is your default. You're retraining a habit of a lifetime — be patient with it. `Free`
- 📋 **Box breathing (4-4-4-4).** Breathe in for **4**, hold for **4**, out for **4**, hold empty for **4**. Repeat for 1–5 minutes. A simple square of breath, popular with people who work under pressure. Use it before a hard conversation, a presentation, a tee shot — anywhere you need calm with your eyes open and your wits kept. Calm and sharp are not opposites.
- **[The sigh / cyclic sighing — Balban et al., Cell Reports Medicine (2023)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36630953/)** — The quickest mood lever in this guide. Two inhales through the nose — one full, then a second short one on top that tops the lungs right off — followed by one long, slow exhale through the mouth until empty. That's one sigh. Your body already does this on its own every few minutes and right before sleep; the technique is simply doing it on purpose. In a Stanford study, five minutes a day of sigh-style breathing for a month lifted people's mood. One to three sighs work in the moment, anywhere. `Free`
- **[Coherent breathing, ~5.5 breaths a minute — Zaccaro et al., Frontiers in Human Neuroscience (2018)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30245619/)** — One steady, slow pace: roughly **5–6 counts in, 5–6 counts out, no holds** — near five and a half breaths a minute, around the rate where your heart and breath settle into step with each other. Five to twenty minutes. If you build a daily habit on one pattern, this is a good one to build it on. `Free`
- 📋 **Extended exhale.** The simplest calming rule there is: **make the out-breath longer than the in-breath** — in for 4, out for 6 or 8, no holds. Exhaling is the half of the cycle that slows the heart. No app, no count beyond that ratio. Once you've practiced it at your desk, you can use it mid-sentence in a meeting and nobody will know.
- 📋 **Alternate-nostril breathing.** From yoga's breath-control tradition. Sit tall. Close the right nostril with your thumb and breathe in through the left. Close both briefly, then release the right and breathe out through it. In through the right, close, out through the left. That's one round; do 5–10. What it reliably does is make you breathe slowly and on purpose — which is most of the benefit. Skip it if your nose is blocked; forcing it defeats the purpose.
- **[4-7-8 breathing (the Relaxing Breath) — Dr. Andrew Weil](https://www.drweil.com/health-wellness/body-mind-spirit/stress-anxiety/three-breathing-exercises-and-techniques/26378331/)** — Taught the way its source teaches it. Tip of the tongue on the ridge just behind your upper front teeth, kept there throughout. (1) Breathe out completely through the mouth with a whoosh. (2) Close the mouth, breathe in quietly through the nose to a count of **4**. (3) Hold for **7**. (4) Out through the mouth, whoosh, for **8**. That's one cycle — do **four cycles total**, twice a day. The **ratio** is what matters, not the seconds — if the hold feels hard, speed the whole thing up evenly. Dr. Weil adapted it from pranayama, yoga's ancient breath control. A lovely lights-out ritual. `Free`
- 📋 **Strong breathwork (Wim Hof style).** Rounds of fast, deep breathing, then holding the breath out on empty lungs — often paired with cold showers or cold water. It's the intense end of the pool, and some people swear by it. Two calm rules if you try it: only sitting or lying down, on dry land — and never in water, never before swimming, never while driving, because strong breathing can make you lightheaded, and that's fine on a couch and dangerous in a pool. If you're pregnant, or have heart, blood-pressure, or seizure concerns, check with a doctor first — or simply enjoy the gentle patterns above. They carry most of the benefit anyway.

## 🙏 Gratitude

The smallest practice in this guide. A few minutes of noticing what's good, and who helped. Small, real, and worth having.

- **[Counting blessings vs. burdens — Emmons & McCullough (2003)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12585811/)** — The founding studies of the field. People who listed things they were grateful for each week felt better about their lives than people who listed hassles or everyday events. The practice: once a week, write down up to five things you're grateful for — one line each on why. `Free`
- **[Three good things & the gratitude visit — Seligman et al., American Psychologist (2005)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16045394/)** — Two practices from one well-known study, and both earned their place. **Three good things:** each night, write three things that went well and why — in the study, the lift lasted for months. **The gratitude visit:** write a thank-you letter to someone who changed your life, and deliver it in person — the biggest single boost of anything tested. The visit fades; the nightly habit lasts. Small and repeated beats big and once. `Free`
- **[The gratitude letter, step by step — Greater Good in Action (UC Berkeley)](https://ggia.berkeley.edu/practice/gratitude_letter)** — The gratitude visit as a ready-to-run practice, from Berkeley's Greater Good Science Center. About 10 minutes to write, 30 for the visit. Pick someone you never properly thanked. Write directly to them, and be specific about what they did and what it changed — specific is what makes it land, for them and for you. Deliver it in person if you possibly can. Yes, it's awkward. Do it anyway. `Free`
- 📋 **Keep it fresh.** Here's the classic way this practice goes stale: "coffee, bed, dog," every day, until the list means nothing. The fixes are simple. **Rotate the lens** — a person, a place, a small pleasure, something that *didn't* go wrong. **Go specific** — not "my brother" but "my brother drove over with a ladder without being asked." **Include the why** — what caused each good thing. A practice you keep beats a practice you perfect.
- 📋 **Gratitude is not pretending.** Nothing here asks you to call hard things good, deny that something hurt, or perform thankfulness for an audience. The practice is bookkeeping, not acting — the good entries go in the ledger next to the real ones, not on top of them. You can be grateful and angry at the same time. Honest people often are.

## 🧩 A Simple Week

One small thing a day. Nothing here takes more than ten minutes.

- 📋 **The 7-day starter plan.** **Day 1:** the 2-minute starter practice from [Start Here](#-start-here). **Day 2:** a 5-minute guided body scan (free recordings at UCLA MARC, in [Free Tools](#-free-tools)). **Day 3:** sighs — 5 minutes of sigh-paced breathing, or one deliberate sigh each time stress spikes. **Day 4:** a 10-minute walking meditation. **Day 5:** three good things, written, with the why for each. **Day 6:** 5 minutes of coherent breathing, or 5 minutes of loving-kindness. **Day 7:** repeat your favorite from the week, at 10 minutes. Then keep the two or three that earned it. The rest will wait for you — they've waited a few thousand years already.
- 📋 **The morning stack (10 minutes).** Three sighs on waking → 5 minutes of focused attention → one line of gratitude, written or spoken, specific. Breath first, because it works before motivation wakes up. Gratitude last, because the day hasn't had a chance to argue yet.
- 📋 **The workday reset (2 minutes, eyes open).** Before a hard meeting: four rounds of box breathing. Mid-afternoon slump: stand up, walk, and run long exhales — in for 4, out for 8 — for one minute. Neither needs a closed door or an explanation. A practice you don't have to hide is a practice you'll keep.
- 📋 **The evening stack (in bed).** Three good things on paper or in a notes app. Then 4-7-8 breathing, four rounds, lights out. If your mind is still racing after ten minutes, put on a body scan recording and let someone else's voice do the pacing. There's no prize for white-knuckling the dark.
- 📋 **Anchor it to something.** Attach the practice to something that already happens every day: after the coffee starts brewing, after the seatbelt clicks, after brushing your teeth. "After X, I do two minutes" survives busy weeks that kill "I'll meditate sometime today." And when a day goes sideways, shrink the dose before you skip the day — two minutes counts.
- 📋 **"My mind won't sit still."** Good news: that *is* the workout. A wandering mind isn't a broken meditation — noticing the wander and returning is the exact rep. Practical fixes: shorten the session, open your eyes, count the exhales (counting gives the mind a railing), or move — walking meditation and tai chi count fully. There's no such thing as being bad at this. There's only dose and fit, and both can be adjusted.

## 🆓 Free Tools

An app is a timer, a library, and a reminder. Handy — never required. Every technique in this guide is free, and the free tools come first.

- **[Mindfulness Coach — VA National Center for PTSD](https://www.ptsd.va.gov/appvid/mobile/mindfulcoach_app.asp)** — If you download one app from this guide, start here. A complete, genuinely free meditation app from the Department of Veterans Affairs, built for veterans and open to everyone: guided training, audio exercises, progress tracking — and it takes and shares no personal information, and has nothing to sell you, because it's the VA, not a startup. `Free`
- **[Healthy Minds Program — Healthy Minds Innovations](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.healthyminds&hl=en_US&gl=US)** — The science-first app, built by the nonprofit arm of the University of Wisconsin–Madison's Center for Healthy Minds. It teaches four trainable skills — Awareness, Connection, Insight, Purpose — through guided practices and short lessons. Free means free here: donor-funded, no subscription, no ads. `Free`
- **[UCLA Mindful Awareness Research Center (MARC)](https://www.uclahealth.org/marc/)** — A university research center giving the product away: free guided meditations in English and Spanish (breathing, body scan, loving-kindness, sleep), free weekly drop-in sessions, and the free UCLA Mindful app. No account, no premium tier. The recordings are plain and unglamorous. Good. `Free`
- **[Palouse Mindfulness — the free online MBSR course](https://palousemindfulness.com)** — The full 8-week Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction course online: every video, reading, and guided meditation from the live class, self-paced, put online free by a certified MBSR instructor. No fees, no registration, no email required. Also listed under [Ways to Meditate](#-ways-to-meditate) — it earns both spots. `Free`
- 📋 **Insight Timer — the giant free library.** The core offer is real and generous: a library of 300,000+ guided meditations, talks, and music tracks, plus a proper meditation timer — free, no credit card. Some structured courses and offline downloads sit behind its optional subscription (about $60/year in the US). The free library alone is more than most people will ever need. Quality varies by teacher, because anyone can publish — the university and VA sources above are the curated end of the pool. `Free` (core) / `Paid` (courses & offline)
- **[Waking Up — the scholarship page](https://app.wakingup.com/scholarship)** — Sam Harris's app: a 28-day introductory course plus a deep library of guided practice and talks, for people who want the philosophy-of-mind layer with their meditation. Paid, at the top of the market (about $130/year on the web). It earns its place for one unusual policy: **anyone who can't afford it can request a free year — no questions asked** — through the scholarship page linked here. If money is the obstacle, take the scholarship. That's what it's for. `Paid` (free on request)
- 📋 **Headspace — the polished beginner course.** Friendly, structured, genuinely good at teaching the basics in order. Almost everything sits behind the subscription (about $70/year or $13/month in the US, with a free trial on the annual plan). Fine if you'll use it daily; expensive wallpaper if you won't. And if the subscription ever lapses, remember: every technique it teaches is in this guide, free. `Paid`
- 📋 **Calm — the sleep app.** Know what you're buying: sleep stories, soundscapes, and bedtime content are its real product, and for winding down at night it's well made. A small sampler stays free; the full library is a subscription, roughly $70–80/year after a trial. Before you pay for narration, compare the free sleep tools above — sometimes the free voice is the one that works. `Paid`
- 📋 **The tools rule.** If an app charges monthly for a gratitude journal or locks box breathing behind a paywall, close it. Pay only for convenience you'll actually use: a voice you like, a course structure that keeps you honest, offline access. Never pay because the free version was designed to frustrate you.

## 🛒 Before You Buy Anything

Every technique in this guide is free. What's sold in this space is packaging — sometimes honest, sometimes not. Two minutes here can save you real money.

- **[When a Business Offer or Coaching Program Is a Scam — FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/when-business-offer-or-coaching-program-scam)** — The Federal Trade Commission's page on coaching schemes lists the tells: guaranteed results, pressure to act *now*, testimonials in place of evidence, and requests for more money after you've already paid. Wellness gurus run the same script with better lighting — swap "income" for "transformation" and every line still scans. Once you've seen the script, you can't unsee it. `Free`
- 📋 **The funnel, in one line.** Free video → free "masterclass" (a sales pitch with breathing breaks) → paid course → retreat → five-figure "certification." At every step, the product is the *next* step. A good teacher's success is that you need them less over time. A funnel's success is that you never stop paying.
- 📋 **Big promises are the tell.** "Detox your trauma in one session." "Rewire your brain in 8 weeks." "Ancient secret they don't want you to know." If a claim sounds like magic, it isn't this. The real practices are quiet, gradual, and free — and they're all in this guide.

## 🌿 Gentle Notes

Four short notes, kept small on purpose. This guide is meant to lower your heart rate, not raise it.

- 📋 **Go at your own pace.** Shorter is fine. Eyes open is fine. Stopping a session whenever you want is fine — you are always allowed to stop. Some days, sitting quietly will feel easy; some days it won't, and both are normal. This is rest, not a test.
- 📋 **Strong breathing, two rules.** Only sitting or lying down, on dry land. Never in water, never before swimming, never while driving. That's the whole safety section — everything gentle in this guide is fine anywhere, any time.
- 📋 **A companion, not a replacement.** These practices sit happily alongside a doctor or therapist — they don't replace one, and they're not meant to. If you're carrying something heavy, let a professional help you carry it. Asking for that help is a strength, not a detour.
- 📋 **If you're ever in crisis.** In the US, call or text **988** — the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, free, day or night. That's the right tool for that moment, and reaching for it is the brave move.
- **[Meditation and Mindfulness — NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/meditation-and-mindfulness-effectiveness-and-safety)** — Want to read more? The National Institutes of Health keeps a calm, plain-language page on what meditation and mindfulness are, and what researchers have found so far — help with stress, mood, sleep, and pain for many people. A good next read, from a source that isn't selling anything. `Free`
